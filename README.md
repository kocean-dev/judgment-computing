# Judgment Computing

## The Final Question of AI Computing

**Version 1.1.0**  
October 2026  
YL Khim

> A manifesto exploring the possibility that the next stage of computing beyond Intent Computing may be Judgment Computing.
>
> **Judgment is not our invention.**  
> **Judgment is our discovery.**

---

# Preface

This document is not a technical specification.

It is not a research paper, nor was it written to describe a specific product or system.

It was written to record a question.

That question did not begin with artificial intelligence.

It began with something far simpler:

> Humans do not remember windows. They remember the work they were doing.

For decades, computing systems have evolved around files, applications, processes, and windows.

Yet humans think fundamentally in terms of work.

As computing is reconsidered through the lens of work, the problem of context emerges.

In attempting to understand context, the problem of intent appears.

In extending intent into an executable system, questions of authority, verification, and governance inevitably follow.

At the end of that chain of reasoning, one question remains:

> Will sufficiently powerful systems ultimately encounter the problem of judgment?

This document is a record of that question.

**This document was not written to predict the future.**

**It was written to preserve a question before the future arrives.**

---

# The Evolution of Computing

The history of computing is a history of abstraction.

Early computers executed commands.

Applications later emerged, allowing humans to select functions instead of writing instructions directly.

Workflow systems connected those functions into processes.

Today we are approaching another transition.

Humans are gradually moving from describing procedures to describing intentions.

Systems increasingly attempt to understand desired outcomes and determine for themselves how those outcomes can be achieved.

Many people refer to this transition as **Intent Computing**.

Yet the moment intent becomes executable, computing is no longer merely a problem of execution.

It becomes a problem of authority.

---

# Intent Creates Authority

Powerful systems do more than compute.

They read information, communicate, control resources, and act on behalf of human beings.

The important question is not what a system can do.

The important question is why it is allowed to do it.

We propose a principle:

> Intent creates authority.

Authority should not emerge from capability.

It should not emerge from intelligence.

Authority should emerge only from authorized intent.

No system should possess authority beyond the intent it has been delegated to fulfill.

As systems become increasingly capable of executing intent, the central problem of computing may shift from execution itself to the structure of authority.

---

# Intent Requires Contract

Intent alone is not sufficient.

Human intent is often ambiguous and incomplete.

The same objective can carry entirely different meanings depending on context.

For that reason, systems should not guess intent.

Actions should arise from agreement, not assumption.

We call this explicit agreement an **Intent Contract**.

Future systems should not be systems that speculate on behalf of their users.

They should be systems that operate within mutually agreed boundaries.

Authority originates from intent.

Intent is legitimized through contract.

---

# From Trust to Verification

The more authority a system possesses, the less trust alone is sufficient.

Human societies developed records, accounting, auditing, and law not because trust was unimportant, but because trust alone was inadequate.

Computing may evolve in the same direction.

Actions should be explainable.

Authority should be auditable.

Decisions should be traceable.

Future systems may be required not merely to be trustworthy, but to be verifiable.

> Trust may be optional. Verification is essential.

---

# Governance and Constitution

If verification confirms actions, governance constrains them.

Reality, however, is not composed solely of simple rules.

Policies conflict.

Principles conflict.

Values conflict.

The same rule does not apply correctly to every situation.

Sufficiently powerful systems may therefore require something beyond a collection of rules.

We call that a **Constitution**.

A constitution is not merely another rule.

A constitution defines priorities.

- Which values take precedence?
- Which principles matter most?
- Which boundaries must never be crossed?

If governance manages behavior, a constitution constrains governance itself.

What we may ultimately need is not more rules, but clearer priorities.

---

# Possible Structures of Judgment

If judgment is indeed a computable problem, future systems may require structures beyond execution, intent, verification, and governance.

We do not claim that such structures already exist.

We merely suggest that sufficiently autonomous systems may eventually require mechanisms capable of addressing conflicts that rules alone cannot resolve.

Such structures might include:

### Constitution

A constitution defines foundational priorities and non-negotiable boundaries.

It is not merely a collection of rules.

It establishes which values take precedence when multiple principles come into conflict.

### Governance

Governance defines operational policies and behavioral constraints.

If a constitution defines priorities, governance applies those priorities to the operation of a system.

### Conflict Resolution

Rules do not eliminate conflict.

Goals compete.

Principles collide.

Values may point toward different conclusions.

Future systems may therefore require mechanisms capable of identifying, evaluating, and reconciling competing objectives.

### Human Final Authority

Judgment ultimately raises the question of responsibility.

No matter how capable a system becomes, responsibility cannot be delegated entirely to machines.

Human beings must remain the final authority.

---

These concepts are not presented here as a complete framework.

Nor are they intended as a proposed architecture for future systems.

They are merely examples of the kinds of structures that may become necessary if computing ultimately encounters the problem of judgment.

Rules may guide action.

Governance may constrain action.

Yet neither necessarily determines which principle should prevail when principles themselves come into conflict.

The form such systems may eventually take remains unknown.

What matters here is not the structure itself.

What matters is the possibility that judgment may require structures beyond those that computing has traditionally relied upon.

---

# Physics Over Policy

Most systems attempt to prohibit dangerous actions.

Yet prohibited actions and impossible actions are not the same.

A prohibited action can still occur.

A structurally impossible action cannot.

We therefore propose another principle:

> Physics Over Policy

A well-designed system does not merely forbid dangerous behavior after the fact.

It makes such behavior structurally impossible.

---

# The Discovery of Judgment

As computing grows more powerful, a recurring pattern emerges.

Rules encounter exceptions.

Policies encounter conflicts.

Principles encounter contradictions.

Values compete with one another.

These problems do not disappear.

They simply move to higher levels of abstraction.

Eventually every conflict converges into a single question:

- Which value should take priority?
- Which principle matters more?
- Which decision is appropriate within the present context?

We call this problem **Judgment**.

Judgment is not the absence of rules.

Judgment is the process of reconciling values, principles, and context when rules alone are insufficient.

We do not claim to have solved judgment.

We do not claim to have defined it completely.

We merely suggest that sufficiently advanced systems will ultimately encounter it.

And this is the central claim of this manifesto:

> Judgment is not our invention.  
> Judgment is our discovery.

---

# The Principle of Human Final Authority

Judgment ultimately leads to the question of responsibility.

- Who is accountable for a decision?
- Who bears its consequences?
- Who possesses final authority?

Our answer is straightforward.

AI may advise.

AI may analyze.

AI may assist in execution.

However, authority and responsibility cannot be separated.

Therefore:

> Final authority must remain with human beings.

---

# Note on Scope

This document does not present a complete theory.

It does not provide a complete implementation.

Nor does it explain how judgment can be computed, verified, or formally represented.

Instead, it proposes a hypothesis:

> As computing systems gain greater autonomy, the defining challenge of the future may not be capability, but authority.

And at the end of that path, we may encounter the question of judgment.

---

# Conclusion

Computing learned how to execute commands.

Computing learned how to manage applications and workflows.

It is now learning how to execute intent.

What comes next remains unknown.

Yet one possibility is becoming increasingly difficult to ignore.

As systems gain greater autonomy, execution alone is no longer sufficient.

We must discuss authority.

We must discuss governance.

We must discuss verification.

We must discuss responsibility.

And ultimately,

> We must discuss judgment.

The evolution of computing may follow a path such as this:

```text
Command Computing
        ↓
Application Computing
        ↓
Workflow Computing
        ↓
Intent Computing
        ↓
Judgment Computing ?
```

---

# The Final Question

We learned how to compute commands.

We learned how to compute processes.

We are learning how to compute intent.

The remaining question is both technical and philosophical:

> Can judgment be computed?

# About the Author

YL Khim is neither a computer scientist, an academic researcher, nor an AI expert.

He began with a simple question:

> Are humans using computers, or are they simply trying to accomplish work?

In reimagining computing around work, successive questions emerged concerning context, intent, authority, governance, verification, constitution, and ultimately judgment.

This manifesto is not a final answer to those questions.

It is an intermediate record of an unfinished exploration.

And that exploration currently rests on a single question:

> Can judgment be computed?
